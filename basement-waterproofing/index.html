Three questions that decide who you need

Where does the water appear, when does it appear and how much comes in? Those three answers sort most wet basements. Water at the floor and wall joint after rain is usually groundwater pressure, and a drainage system can handle it. A horizontal crack with a wall bowing inward is a structural issue.

Water that shows up on a dry day with no rain points away from groundwater altogether. That is the pattern of a plumbing leak, a failing water heater or a condensation problem, and leak detection is the right first step.

What drainage work involves

The pieces below are the parts of a basement drainage system a plumber works on.

01

Interior drain tile

A perimeter channel along the footing collects water that seeps through the wall or floor joint and carries it to the sump pit. Installing it means cutting a trench in the slab, laying perforated pipe in gravel and patching the floor. It manages water entering at the base but does not fix a cracked wall.

02

Sump pit and pump

The pit is where collected water gathers before the pump sends it out. Pit size, pump capacity and the float setting affect how often it cycles. Existing pits are covered under sump pump repair when the pump fails during a storm.

03

Discharge line

A pump that sends water five feet from the house and lets it soak back in is working against itself. The outlet needs a clear path, a check valve and protection from freezing. Check town rules on where a sump discharge may go before changing the route.

04

Backup power

Storms that bring heavy rain often knock out power, and a pump without power does nothing. A battery backup keeps the pump working for the outage, within the battery's limits.

What to fix outside before spending inside

Grading and gutters cost less to fix than drain tile and often cut the volume of water the basement has to handle. Downspouts that empty near the foundation, soil that slopes toward the house and window wells without covers all feed groundwater next to the wall.

Much of Avon was built during rapid growth after 2000, and backfill around newer foundations can settle over the first years, leaving a dip along the wall. Check for low spots against the house before you pay for interior work. Fixing the surface often reduces what the drains have to carry.

What no plumber should promise

No interior system stops water from touching the foundation. Interior drainage manages water that has already come through, and it only works if the pit, pump and discharge are sized and kept running. If a contractor promises a bone-dry basement without asking where the water enters, ask more questions.

Stormwater and the town

Avon has a stormwater utility, and its fact sheet notes the town has seen significant population growth. Runoff from roofs and driveways has to go somewhere, so where your sump discharges matters to neighbors and to the storm system. Check town rules on where a sump discharge may go, and ask the Avon Planning and Building Department whether your work needs a permit, since rules vary by job.

What to note before we visit

A short list makes the visit more useful.

  • Where water appears: wall, floor joint, window, or near a pipe.
  • Whether it follows rain or appears on dry days.
  • How high and how far it spreads.
  • Whether you already have a sump pit and pump, and how old it is.
  • Where downspouts and the sump discharge empty outside.

Codes, licensing and 811

Plumbing work in Indiana follows the state plumbing code (675 IAC 16), and the Indiana Plumbing Commission licenses plumbing contractors and journeyman plumbers. You can check any plumber through the license lookup at mylicense.in.gov before work starts, and you should call 811 before any digging.

Codes and standards decide what is allowed on a given job, so ask which ones apply to yours.
