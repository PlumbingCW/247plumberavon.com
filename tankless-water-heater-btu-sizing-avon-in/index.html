How Many BTU Does a Tankless Water Heater Need in Avon, IN?

By the Avon 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 30, 2026 · Updated October 30, 2026

Sizing a tankless water heater comes down to one multiplication: flow rate in gallons per minute, times the temperature rise you need, times 500. That gives the BTU per hour the unit must deliver as heat. Divide by the unit's efficiency to get the gas input rating.

The harder part is picking the right flow rate and checking that your gas line and vent can carry the load, which is what this guide walks through.

The formula and why it works

One BTU raises one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. A gallon of water weighs about 8.34 pounds, and a gallon-per-minute flow is 60 gallons an hour, so heating one gallon per minute by one degree takes roughly 500 BTU per hour.

That is where the 500 comes from. Multiply your flow in gallons per minute by the temperature rise in degrees and by 500, and you have the heat output required. A rated input is higher, because no burner turns all its fuel into water heat.

Four steps to a sizing number

Work through them in order, using your own fixtures.

  1. 1

    List what runs at the same time

    Add up the peak flow of the fixtures that are likely to run together, such as two showers and a kitchen tap. Do not add every fixture in the house.

  2. 2

    Find your temperature rise

    Subtract the incoming cold water temperature from the target output temperature. Cold water is colder in winter, so size for your coldest month, and measure your own tap or ask your installer.

  3. 3

    Multiply to get required output

    Flow times rise times 500 gives BTU per hour of heat that must reach the water.

  4. 4

    Divide by efficiency to get input

    Divide the output by the unit's efficiency, expressed as a decimal. A condensing unit near 0.9 to 0.95 needs a bit more input than the output figure.

Typical fixture flow rates to start from

These are common planning figures. Check the label on your own fixtures, since older ones can run higher.

Most showers mix hot and cold, so the heater delivers less than the full showerhead flow, but plan with the full figure to leave margin.

A worked example

Say two showers at about 2.0 gpm each and a kitchen tap at about 1.5 gpm run at once, for 5.5 gpm of hot water. If the cold water is around 55 degrees and you want 115, the rise is 60 degrees. Then 5.5 x 60 x 500 gives about 165,000 BTU per hour of output.

At roughly 94 percent efficiency, that points to a unit with an input near 175,000 BTU per hour. This is an illustration, not a recommendation, because your flow, rise and fixtures will differ.

For a sizing check on your own house, call and list the showers, tubs and appliances that tend to run at the same time.

The gas line is the usual surprise

A tank-type gas heater fires at a modest rate, but a tankless unit calls for its full input all at once. Natural gas carries roughly 1,000 BTU per cubic foot, so a large unit pulls a lot of volume at the same time as the furnace and range.

Many homes need a larger gas supply pipe, a different regulator or both. Ask your gas utility whether the meter and service line can carry the added load. If you smell gas at any time, leave the building and call 911 or the gas utility first.

Venting and installation options

The vent type changes where the heater can go and what it costs to install.

01

Condensing units

These recover extra heat from the exhaust and run cool enough to vent through plastic pipe. They also produce acidic condensate that needs a drain and, in many cases, a neutralizer.

02

Non-condensing units

These run hotter exhaust that needs stainless steel venting rated for the purpose. Efficiency is lower, and the vent is more expensive and heavier.

03

Air supply

A heater in a closet or small room needs enough combustion air. Direct-vent models draw outside air through a second pipe, which avoids the problem.

04

Power and condensate

Even gas tankless units need an electrical connection for fans and controls. Plan for an outlet and a drain nearby.

Avon note: hard water, scale and your tankless heater

Citizens Water reports system-wide hardness averaging about 19 grains per gallon, and it varies by treatment plant, so ask Citizens for the figure at your address. Hardness leaves scale inside the narrow passages of a tankless heat exchanger, which lowers efficiency and can cause error codes.

Plan on isolation valves so the unit can be flushed with a descaling solution on a regular schedule, and ask your installer about a sediment filter or water conditioning. The Town of Avon lists CenterPoint Energy for gas, so that utility is the right contact for questions about meter capacity, and the Avon Planning and Building Department can tell you what permit your job needs.

What you can do yourself versus when to call

Sizing is a homeowner exercise. Installation is not.

  • You can list fixtures, measure flow with a bucket and a timer, and note peak simultaneous use.
  • You can flush a tankless unit if the isolation valves are installed and the instructions are clear.
  • Call for gas piping, regulator work, venting and any electrical connection.
  • Call if the unit shows an error code that returns after a flush.
  • Call if the unit will not light or runs cold and hot in turns.

Codes, licensing and 811

Plumbing work in Indiana follows the state plumbing code (675 IAC 16), and the Indiana Plumbing Commission licenses plumbing contractors and journeyman plumbers. You can check any plumber through the license lookup at mylicense.in.gov before work starts, and you should call 811 before any digging.

Codes and standards decide what is allowed on a given job, so ask which ones apply to yours.
