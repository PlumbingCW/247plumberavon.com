Where the new heater goes matters more than the model

Location drives nearly everything else. A heater in a garage needs protection from vehicle impact and room for the flue. One in a utility closet needs combustion air if it burns gas, and one in a finished area needs a drain pan piped to a safe discharge point.

Moving a heater adds new gas, water and vent runs, so a like-for-like swap is the simplest install. Much of Avon was built during rapid growth after 2000, so many homes have a heater in a garage corner or utility room with the connections already in place. For those homes an in-place swap is usually the cleaner plan.

Venting options and what each needs

Gas heaters must exhaust combustion gases safely, and the vent type depends on the unit.

01

Atmospheric draft

A conventional gas tank uses a metal flue rising through the roof, with combustion air drawn from the room. Draft depends on the flue height, slope and clearances to combustibles, so the plumber checks the existing flue for corrosion, loose joints and a blocked cap.

02

Power vent

A power-vent heater has a blower that pushes exhaust through PVC or similar pipe to a sidewall. This allows a heater far from a chimney, but it needs a nearby electrical outlet and a proper termination clear of windows and doors.

03

Direct vent

Direct-vent units draw air from outside and exhaust outside through sealed pipes. They suit tight, sealed homes where room air is limited. Pipe size and maximum length come from the manufacturer's instructions, which the installer follows.

04

Electric heaters

Electric tanks need no vent, which gives more freedom in placement. They need a dedicated circuit sized to the heater's nameplate, with the right breaker and wire gauge.

Expansion tanks and why the plumbing may need one

Water expands when it heats. In a plumbing system with a check valve or backflow preventer, or a pressure-reducing valve at the main, that extra volume has nowhere to go, so pressure spikes can force the relief valve to drip or stress fittings.

An expansion tank is a small pressurized vessel that absorbs the swing. The plumber measures static pressure with a gauge and sets the tank's air charge to match, which is why this part is a calculation rather than an upsell.

Before the installer arrives

A little preparation shortens the visit.

  • Clear a path and a working space of a few feet around the old heater.
  • Find the main water shutoff and the gas shutoff, and make sure each turns.
  • Note the tank capacity, fuel type and any model label you can read.
  • Remove stored items from around the heater and from the drain pan area.
  • Tell us if the heater serves a soaking tub, a second bath or a recirculation line.
  • If you smell gas at any time, leave and call 911 or CenterPoint Energy at 1-800-227-1376.

Tell us the current heater's type and location, and we will walk through what the new install needs.

Gas line, electrical and haul-away

Gas lines must be sized for the total load on the home, and the plumber checks that the line serving the heater has the capacity, a shutoff within reach and a sediment trap before the connection. The new connection is leak tested before the unit is lit.

The old tank is drained, disconnected and carried out, so haul-away is part of the job. Mention the stairs, a finished basement or a tight crawl before the visit.

Code, permits and inspection in Avon

Indiana plumbing work follows the state plumbing code, 675 IAC 16. The Town of Avon Planning and Building Department takes permit applications through its online portal and sits at 6570 East US Highway 36. Permit rules vary by job, so ask the department for yours at 317-272-0948 before the install is scheduled.

The code also affects the details you can see: a discharge pipe off the relief valve, a drain pan where leaks would damage finished floors, and clearances to combustibles.
