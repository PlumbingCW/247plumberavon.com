When a camera look is the right first step

Guessing about a sewer line is expensive in both directions. Digging up a pipe that is fine wastes a yard, and ignoring a pipe that is failing invites a basement backup. A camera replaces the guess with footage of the actual wall, joints and slope.

The inspection is useful when you are buying a house, when drains clog repeatedly, when a sewer odor shows up with no obvious source, or before you plan any excavation, tree removal or landscaping near the line.

Good reasons to schedule an inspection

Any one of these is enough.

  • You are buying a home and want to know what is under the lawn before closing.
  • A drain has been cleaned more than once and keeps returning.
  • A soggy, sunken or unusually green patch of yard has no clear water source.
  • Several fixtures drain slowly with no single clog to blame.
  • You plan to remodel a basement bathroom or pour concrete over a line.
  • A tree near the lateral has been removed or has grown large.
  • You are considering pipe lining and need to know if the pipe qualifies.

What happens on inspection day

The visit is short and the sequence is simple.

  1. 1

    Find the entry point

    The plumber uses a cleanout, or removes a toilet if no cleanout is reachable, so the camera can enter the main drain.

  2. 2

    Clear anything blocking the view

    A line coated with grease or full of debris hides the pipe wall. A quick cleaning pass may be needed first.

  3. 3

    Feed the camera down the line

    A flexible rod pushes the camera head along the pipe while a monitor shows the wall and the footage counter shows distance.

  4. 4

    Trace and mark

    A transmitter in the camera head lets the plumber find any defect from above ground and mark its position and depth.

  5. 5

    Review the findings

    The plumber explains what the footage shows, what it means and whether action is needed now or can wait.

Common findings and what they mean

The camera tends to find a handful of patterns.

01

Root intrusion

Fine roots entering a joint look like hair or fur on the pipe wall. They signal a joint that leaks and will keep admitting roots. Cutting buys time and lining or spot repair addresses the joint.

02

Belly or standing water

A low spot holds water and solids. On the footage, the picture goes underwater at the dip. A belly usually needs a section dug and re-graded, since lining cannot correct slope.

03

Offset or separated joint

Pipe ends that no longer line up catch paper and let soil in. Small offsets may be monitored, while larger ones usually need a spot repair.

04

Cracks, pitting and scale

Cracks show as dark lines, and old iron shows rough, scaled walls. The extent decides whether cleaning, lining or replacement fits.

Buying a home: what an inspection can do

A sewer camera inspection is separate from a general home inspection, and many general inspectors do not run a camera. If you are under contract, check how much time your contract gives you for inspections and schedule early. Your agent can confirm the dates.

Footage can show a failing lateral that a seller may not know about. That information supports a repair request, a price discussion or a decision to walk away, and the choice is yours.

What affects the cost of an inspection

The price follows the work involved, not the footage.

Access

A cleanout makes the inspection direct. Pulling a toilet to reach the line adds time.

Line length

A longer run takes more time and may need a second entry point.

Cleaning first

A coated line needs a cleaning pass before the camera can see anything.

Locating

Tracing a defect above ground and marking its depth adds equipment and time.

Recording

Ask when you call whether a copy of the footage is available for your records.

Homeowners policies typically respond to sudden and accidental damage and typically exclude wear, gradual leaks and the failed part itself, so ask your carrier. An inspection is a diagnostic, not a repair, so most policies do not cover it.

Codes, licensing and 811

Plumbing work in Indiana follows the state plumbing code (675 IAC 16), and the Indiana Plumbing Commission licenses plumbing contractors and journeyman plumbers. You can check any plumber through the license lookup at mylicense.in.gov before work starts, and you should call 811 before any digging.

Codes and standards decide what is allowed on a given job, so ask which ones apply to yours.
