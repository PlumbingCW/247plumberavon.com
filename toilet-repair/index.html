Toilet overflowing right now: what to do first

Most overflows can be slowed in under a minute if you know where to reach. Do these in order while you call.

  • Lift the tank lid and push the flapper down, or reach behind the bowl and turn the oval supply valve clockwise until it stops.
  • Do not flush again. A second flush on a blocked line adds more water to a bowl that is already full.
  • Put towels at the base and move rugs, cabinets and anything electrical away from the wet area.
  • If water is coming from a floor drain, a tub or a second toilet, stop using every fixture in the house and tell us. That pattern points to the main drain, not this toilet.
  • If the supply valve will not turn, shut off the water at the main valve where the line enters the house and call.

Running toilet: finding the leak you cannot see

A running toilet is usually the quietest way to waste water in a house. The flapper at the bottom of the tank no longer seals, so water slips into the bowl and the fill valve tops the tank up again every few minutes.

We confirm it with a dye test. A few drops of food coloring go in the tank, and if color shows in the bowl after ten or fifteen minutes with no flush, the flapper or flush valve seat is leaking. If the tank water is sitting at the top of the overflow tube instead, the fill valve is not shutting off and needs adjusting or replacing.

Both repairs are small when caught early. Left alone, the constant refill is what drives up a Citizens Water bill, and two running toilets in one house are more common than most people expect.

The parts inside the tank, and what each one does

Most repairs happen inside the tank, where four parts do all the work. Knowing them helps you describe the problem on the phone.

01

Fill valve

The fill valve refills the tank after a flush and shuts off when a float reaches the set level. When it fails you hear hissing, the tank never fills, or water runs down the overflow tube.

Replacement is the normal answer because the seals inside wear out together. The new valve is adjusted so the water stops about an inch below the top of the overflow tube.

02

Flapper and flush valve

The rubber flapper lifts to start a flush and drops to seal the tank. Over time it softens, warps or collects mineral crust, and the seal fails.

If the flush valve seat underneath is pitted, a new flapper will not seal either. In that case the whole flush valve assembly is swapped, which means removing the tank from the bowl.

03

Handle, lever and chain

A flush lever that sticks, droops or does nothing is almost always a snapped arm or a chain that has slipped off. These are inexpensive parts and a quick fix.

Chain length matters. Too tight and the flapper never closes, too loose and a full flush will not lift it.

04

Supply valve and line

The shutoff valve on the wall feeds the tank through a short braided or rigid line. Old valves seize, drip at the stem, or refuse to close, which turns every later repair into a bigger one.

Replacing a failed supply valve is a normal part of a toilet visit. It also means you finally have a shutoff that works the next time you need it.

Wax ring and flange: leaks at the base of the toilet

The wax ring is a compressed seal between the toilet outlet and the closet flange on the floor. When it fails, water or sewer gas escapes at the base, and the floor around it turns soft, stained or smelly.

A rocking toilet is the usual cause. Movement breaks the seal, so a new ring alone will not hold unless the flange is sound and the toilet sits solid. We check that the flange is level with the finished floor, that the bolts are tight, and that the flange itself is not cracked or rusted through.

A damaged flange gets a repair ring or a replacement, not another wax ring on top of a bad foundation. If the subfloor feels spongy, we tell you before we set anything, because that is carpentry and sometimes leak damage that deserves a second look.

WaterSense and why a new toilet is not just a swap

EPA WaterSense labels toilets that flush with no more than 1.28 gallons and still meet performance testing. Many older toilets in a house use well over that per flush, so a replacement can lower the water bill every month.

Fit matters as much as the label. The distance from the wall to the center of the floor bolts (the rough-in) and the bowl shape must match the space, which is why we measure before choosing a model.

Hard water and toilet parts in Avon

Citizens Water reports system-wide hardness averaging about 19 grains per gallon, and it varies by treatment plant, so ask Citizens for the figure at your address. Water that hard leaves mineral crust on flappers, clogs the small rim jets under the bowl lip, and shortens the life of fill valve seals.

If your parts keep failing within a few years, or the flush is weak and the rim holes look crusted, that points to water quality rather than bad luck. A softener helps, and our water softener installation page explains how that decision is made.

When the problem is the drain, not the toilet

A toilet that gurgles, drains slowly, or backs up along with a tub or sink is rarely a toilet problem. The blockage is further down, and replacing parts in the toilet will not change anything.

We start with a plunger and closet auger on a single clogged toilet. If more than one fixture is affected, the next step is drain cleaning, and a repeated backup into the lowest drain in the house may need the sewer line looked at with a camera.

Codes, licensing and 811

Plumbing work in Indiana follows the state plumbing code (675 IAC 16), and the Indiana Plumbing Commission licenses plumbing contractors and journeyman plumbers. You can check any plumber through the license lookup at mylicense.in.gov before work starts, and you should call 811 before any digging.

Codes and standards decide what is allowed on a given job, so ask which ones apply to yours.
