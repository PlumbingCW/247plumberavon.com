Reading the pattern: one fixture or the whole house

A single slow sink usually means a clog in the trap or the short branch behind the wall. If the tub gurgles when the washer drains, or a basement floor drain bubbles after someone flushes upstairs, the blockage sits further down, on a shared branch or on the main house drain. The first thing a plumber checks is how many fixtures are affected and in what order.

Drain pipes in a house form a tree. Every fixture has a trap, each trap feeds a branch, and the branches join a main drain that leaves through the foundation toward the sewer lateral. A clog upstream of a junction hurts only the fixtures above it. A clog at the main hurts them all, and the lowest fixture, often a basement shower or floor drain, shows it first.

That is why a drain call starts with questions about where water appears, not with a tool. The answer also tells us whether the job belongs here or on main sewer line cleaning, which uses larger equipment.

When a clogged drain becomes an emergency

Call right away if any of these are happening, and do the following until a plumber arrives.

  • Foul or sewage-colored water is coming up in a tub, shower or floor drain.
  • More than one fixture is backing up at the same time.
  • Water is spreading toward a furnace, a water heater, outlets or finished flooring. Keep people and pets out of that area.
  • Stop every fixture and appliance that drains, including the washer, dishwasher and shower.
  • Leave chemical drain opener on the shelf. It rarely cuts a hard blockage and it is a skin and eye hazard for the next person to open the trap.
  • Do not keep plunging a toilet that is backing up because of a clog somewhere else. The toilet is not the problem.
  • If a fixture keeps filling, close the stop valve under it.

Snaking and cabling: matching the cable to the pipe

Cable cleaning is not one tool. The right one depends on pipe size, how far the clog sits and what it is made of.

01

Hand auger for traps and short branches

A hand-cranked auger about a quarter inch thick reaches the trap and a few feet of branch. It handles hair, soap scum and a dropped object near the fixture. It is the right tool for a bathroom sink and often for a tub.

02

Drain machine for tubs, laundry and branches

A motorized drum machine feeds a steel cable through a cleanout or the fixture opening. The spinning cable hooks or breaks up the blockage. Size matters: a thin cable bends in a larger pipe and misses the clog, and a thick one can kink in a tight trap.

03

Cutter heads for roots and grease

Sectional cable with interchangeable heads lets a plumber cut roots or scrape grease from the pipe wall instead of poking a hole through it. A hole through grease closes again within weeks. Scraping the wall restores the pipe diameter, and diameter is what keeps a line flowing.

04

When cabling stops being the right method

If the cable punches through and the drain slows again soon after, the pipe walls are coated, and hydro jetting fits better because water scours the whole circumference. If the cable stops at the same spot every time, a camera look is smarter than more force on a pipe that may be broken.

Kitchen sink clogs: grease, food solids and the second bay

A kitchen drain collects the worst material in the house: cooking fat, starch, coffee grounds and fibrous scraps. Fat cools on the pipe wall and narrows it a little more with every wash, until a slow drain becomes a standing sink. Hot water moves the fat down the line, where it hardens again past the trap.

Double-bay sinks and disposals add complications. If water from one bay rises in the other, the blockage sits past the joint where they meet. When a disposal is in the line, a plumber works through the trap arm or a cleanout, because pushing cable through the grinder chamber can damage it. A disposal that hums but will not turn is a different problem, covered under garbage disposal repair.

Dishwashers drain through the disposal or the sink trap. A dishwasher that leaves standing water and a sink that gurgles when it runs often share the same partial clog.

When the drain line itself leaks

Some calls that sound like clogs are drain line leaks. Water that shows at a baseboard or behind a shower wall only when a fixture drains, and never when only the supply is on, points to a cracked pipe or a loose joint in the waste line. A slow drain with a damp spot and a sour smell nearby tells the same story.

Cable can make a leaking joint worse, so a plumber checks for dampness and listens before pushing it through. Older cast iron drains tend to rust through along the bottom of the pipe. Those show up in older houses more than in most post-2000 homes around Avon. If the source is not obvious, leak detection covers how a hidden leak gets located.

How a drain cleaning visit runs

The order matters more than the machine.

  1. 1

    Questions before tools

    Which fixtures, in what order, since when, and whether anything unusual went down the drain. Those answers narrow the pipe segment to open.

  2. 2

    Find the access point

    The plumber uses the nearest route to the blockage: the fixture trap, a cleanout, or a removed toilet when nothing else is reachable.

  3. 3

    Clear it, then test the whole system

    After the cable or jetter breaks through, water runs at several fixtures at once. A drain that passes one cup of water can still fail under a full tub.

  4. 4

    Look inside if it keeps returning

    Repeat clogs get a camera on the next visit, because a belly, a root or a cracked joint will not stay cleared by cable alone.

  5. 5

    Explain what was found

    You hear what caused the clog and what, if anything, to change. The price is stated before work starts.

What changes the scope and price of a drain job

No two clogs cost the same, and the drivers are predictable.

Where the clog sits

A trap-level clog is a short job. A blockage deep in a branch or the main takes longer and different equipment.

What the clog is made of

Hair and soap are simple. Roots, hardened grease and collapsed pipe change the method and may call for a camera or a jetter.

Access

A cleanout makes the work quick. With no cleanout, the plumber may need to pull a toilet or work through a roof vent.

Timing

A night, weekend or holiday call can price differently from a planned daytime visit, and the difference is stated before work starts.

Repeat problems

A line that clogs again and again has an underlying cause. Finding it costs more once and less than clearing the same line every few months.

Homeowners policies typically respond to sudden and accidental damage and typically exclude wear, gradual leaks and the failed part itself, so ask your carrier. Some policies sell separate sewer or drain backup coverage, and it helps to know whether you have it before water is on the floor.

What to do while you wait for a drain call

These steps are about safety first and damage control, not repair.

  • Stop running water into the clogged fixture so the line does not overflow.
  • Do not pour liquid drain chemicals into a blockage, because they sit in the pipe and can burn whoever opens it next.
  • If several fixtures gurgle or back up together, avoid flushing and running the washer until the main line is checked.
  • Clear the area around floor drains and move rugs and stored boxes out of reach of any backup.

When cabling is not enough

Cable clears a blockage but it does not clean a pipe. When roots, scale or years of grease have narrowed a line, the better answer is hydro jetting followed by a camera pass to confirm the pipe is intact.

When the problem is the main line itself, the work moves to sewer line cleaning. When the camera shows a crack, a sag or an offset joint, the job becomes a repair question, which sewer line repair explains method by method.

Codes, licensing and 811

Plumbing work in Indiana follows the state plumbing code (675 IAC 16), and the Indiana Plumbing Commission licenses plumbing contractors and journeyman plumbers. You can check any plumber through the license lookup at mylicense.in.gov before work starts, and you should call 811 before any digging.

Codes and standards decide what is allowed on a given job, so ask which ones apply to yours.
