Pit filling in a storm: what to do first

If water is rising in the pit during a storm, the clock is short. Our line is answered 24/7. While you wait, check that the pump is plugged in and its outlet has not tripped, lift the float to see if the pump starts, and bail the pit with a bucket or wet vacuum to buy time.

Stay out of standing water if outlets, power strips or the panel sit near the floor, and shut off power to that area at the breaker if water is close. Move stored boxes, rugs and electronics off the floor. A storm that has already knocked out power also knocks out the pump, which is the case battery backup is built for.

Quick checks before you call

Four of the five common failures show up in a few minutes of looking.

  • Confirm the pump is plugged in and the GFCI outlet has not tripped. A tripped outlet is a common reason for silence after a storm.
  • Lift the float gently. If the pump starts, the float was stuck on the side of the pit or tangled in the cord.
  • Look for debris in the pit. Gravel, mud and fine silt can jam the impeller or hold the float up.
  • Check where the discharge line leaves the house. A frozen, crushed or clogged line sends the water straight back to the pit.
  • Listen for a clunk after the pump shuts off. That is water draining back through a failed check valve.

Repair or replace the pump

A float switch, a check valve or an outlet is usually a repair. A motor that hums without moving water or that has been cycling for years is closer to the end of its life, and replacing it costs less than coming back each storm. Pedestal pumps sit above the pit and are easier to service, while submersible pumps sit in the water and run quieter.

We look at how often the pump runs, how deep the pit is, and whether the pit is big enough to avoid short cycling. A pump that is just too small for the water coming in will keep failing no matter what part we replace. A new basin or a rerouted outlet falls under plumbing installation work, and we will say if your pit needs it.

Sump pump or sewage ejector pump?

A sump pump moves groundwater out of a pit. A sewage ejector pump lifts wastewater from a basement bathroom or laundry up to the main drain, and it works in a sealed basin. They look similar and are not interchangeable, so tell us which you have. A failed ejector means toilets and drains below grade back up, and that is a sanitation problem as well as a flood problem.

Where the discharge goes

A working pump that discharges badly still floods you. The outlet should carry water away from the foundation, not along the wall where it can soak back in. A winter freeze at the end of the line can trap everything behind it.

Avon has a stormwater utility, and its fact sheet notes the town has seen significant population growth. More paved and roofed ground means more runoff to manage, so check town rules on where a sump discharge may go before extending or rerouting the line. The Avon Planning and Building Department is the place to ask.

When the water is not coming from the pump

If the pit is dry or the pump keeps up and the floor is still wet, water is entering somewhere else. Seepage at the wall and floor joint, window wells, a cracked wall or hydrostatic pressure under the slab are drainage problems, and basement waterproofing explains what a plumber handles and when a foundation specialist is the better call.

A wet floor with the pit quiet can also be a plumbing leak. A meter that moves with everything off points to a pipe leak and not the groundwater. We run through both before suggesting any work.

What affects sump pump repair cost

A float swap and a full replacement are very different jobs.

Repair or replacement

A new float or check valve costs less than a new pump with a new basin.

Pump type and size

Submersible, pedestal and higher-capacity pumps differ in cost and in fit for your pit.

Battery backup

A backup system adds equipment and a charger to the job.

Discharge work

Clearing, rerouting or extending the outlet adds time.

Water on the floor

Drying and cleanup are separate from the pump work.

Homeowners policies typically exclude flood and groundwater seepage and often cover sump pump backup only through an added endorsement, so ask your carrier before a storm, not after.

Codes, licensing and 811

Plumbing work in Indiana follows the state plumbing code (675 IAC 16), and the Indiana Plumbing Commission licenses plumbing contractors and journeyman plumbers. You can check any plumber through the license lookup at mylicense.in.gov before work starts, and you should call 811 before any digging.

Codes and standards decide what is allowed on a given job, so ask which ones apply to yours.
